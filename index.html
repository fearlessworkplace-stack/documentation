Overview

What this guide gives you

If someone says, “You need to document,” this is the handoff. It explains why documentation matters, what kinds of records to keep, how to capture facts when you are busy or mobile, how to preserve digital proof, and how to turn all of that into evidence, CYA protection, and a wins file you can use later.

Protection

Documentation protects you when someone rewrites conversations, shifts blame, excludes you, or starts creating confusion around your work.

Preparation

Documentation also prepares you for annual reviews, promotion cases, interviews, résumé updates, and future references.

Clarity

Documentation gives you a timeline so you can stop guessing and start seeing the pattern.

Core rule

Document more than you think you need. Save the wins, the proof, the approvals, the recap emails, the screenshots, and the moments where the story starts changing.

Why it matters

Why you document in the first place

People often start documenting only after the situation gets bad. A stronger approach is to keep records before you need them, because you are building proof, memory, and protection all at once.

Document for protection

  • When someone tries to sabotage your work or reputation.
  • When conversations get retold in ways that leave out what happened.
  • When deadlines, expectations, or responsibilities shift.
  • When you speak up and treatment changes afterward.

Document for your future

  • For annual reviews and self-evaluations.
  • For interviews and résumé bullets.
  • For promotion cases and professional credibility.
  • For your own memory when months have passed.
If you are waiting for a crisis, you are late.

The best documentation habit starts before the conflict. Save proof while work is normal so your record is already strong if something changes later.

Document everything that matters.

You do not need to write a novel about every day. You do need to preserve anything that affects your work, your reputation, your contribution, or your paper trail.

Three parts

The three documentation files

Treat documentation as three connected files. Together they create a record that is fuller and harder to distort.

Evidence file

  • Incidents and patterns
  • Exact words when possible
  • Witnesses
  • Screenshots and chats
  • Timeline of what happened

CYA file

  • Instructions and approvals
  • Scope changes and deadlines
  • Recap emails
  • Submitted work and timestamps
  • Proof that you followed through

Wins file

  • Praise and appreciation
  • Project outcomes and metrics
  • Problems solved
  • Leadership and invisible labor
  • Proof of your credibility
What a CYA file actually is

CYA stands for "cover your ass." It is a record of what you were told to do, by whom, and when, so a verbal instruction cannot later be denied or rewritten. When someone says "let's keep this verbal" or gives you a task with no paper trail, that often leaves no record connecting them to the decision if it goes wrong.

When to start one: Any time you're given an instruction, deadline, or scope change with nothing in writing, especially from someone above you.
The move: Send a short recap right after, in your own words, to the person who gave the instruction. "Per our conversation today, my understanding is that I should [action], because [reason given]. Let me know if I've misunderstood anything."
What it actually does: It protects you in disputes and unemployment claims. It does not guarantee it saves your job, that's a different problem this file isn't built to solve.
How they work together

Your evidence file shows what happened. Your CYA file shows how you handled your work responsibly. Your wins file shows the value and credibility someone may later try to minimize.

In the moment

How to document when you are not at a computer

Not everyone can send recap emails in real time. Nurses, teachers, frontline staff, and people in fast-moving settings need a field method they can use quickly and organize later.

Use a quick-capture method

  • Keep a small notebook, index cards, or a notes app on your phone.
  • Write the date, time, people involved, and one-line fact summary.
  • Add the full details later when you have privacy and time.
  • Use shorthand in the moment if needed, then translate it into a clean entry later.

What to capture first

  • Who was there.
  • What was said or done.
  • What changed because of it.
  • What proof exists or where it may exist.
Fast template: Date. Time. Location. People. One sentence on what happened. One sentence on impact. One note on proof.
Best practice: Capture the facts close to the event, then turn them into a fuller entry later the same day if possible.
Important: Keep your personal documentation off your work computer and off employer-controlled systems when possible. Use personal tools you control, while still handling confidential information carefully and lawfully.
Digital proof

How to document email, Slack, chats, and screenshots

A lot of workplace proof lives in digital fragments. The goal is to preserve the fragments before they disappear, get edited, or become hard to find.

Email

  • Save the full thread, not just one message.
  • Keep the date and sender visible.
  • Use recap emails after verbal conversations.
  • Save approvals, deadlines, and changes in direction.

Slack, Teams, or chat

  • Take screenshots with the timestamp and names visible.
  • Copy the text into your own log if screenshots are not practical.
  • Note the channel, thread, and date.
  • Preserve context so the message makes sense later.
Screenshot rule

When you screenshot, make sure the date, participants, and relevant message are visible. A screenshot with no context is weaker than a screenshot that shows where and when it happened.

Chat rule

If a Slack or Teams message matters, do not assume you will remember it later. Save the image, log the summary, and note what file it belongs in.

Storage rule

Keep your documentation in a place you control. The simplest options are a personal spreadsheet, a secure notes file, a document folder, or a personal system that lets you organize entries, screenshots, and proof together.

Writing

How to write the entry

A strong entry is plain, factual, and useful to a third party. Use the same sequence every time so your record stays readable.

01
State the fact.

What happened, who was involved, and when it happened.

02
Preserve the proof.

Note the screenshot, email, chat, file, or witness that supports the entry.

03
Name the impact.

Explain how it affected the work, access, timing, communication, or reputation.

04
Record the response.

State what you did next, such as clarifying, following up, saving proof, or escalating.

Stronger language

  • “At 3:10 p.m., the deadline changed from Friday to Wednesday in the project meeting.”
  • “The statement was made in front of the team and changed who appeared responsible for the delay.”
  • “I sent a follow-up message and saved the chat screenshot.”

Weaker language

  • “He is always trying to sabotage me.”
  • “She is toxic.”
  • “Everyone knows what is happening.”
