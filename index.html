Start here

What this toolkit gives you

Documentation becomes useful when it does three jobs at once: it preserves what happened, shows how you handled your work, and protects the proof of your contribution. The goal is a record that stays readable even months later.

Protection

Keep a timeline when conversations get rewritten, responsibility shifts, access changes, or your work starts being described differently.

Clarity

Separate one upsetting event from a repeating pattern. Dates and specifics make changes easier to see.

Career record

Preserve praise, outcomes, approvals, and contributions for reviews, interviews, résumé updates, and future references.

Core rule:

Write for the future reader. Assume they were not there. Give them enough detail to understand what occurred, what proof exists, how the work was affected, and what you did next.

Decide what belongs

What is worth documenting?

You do not need a running diary of every irritating interaction. Prioritize events that change the work, the record, your access, your responsibilities, your reputation, or the way you are being managed.

Changes to the work

  • Deadlines or scope changed after agreement
  • Instructions conflict with earlier direction
  • Work is reassigned or removed
  • Necessary access or information is withheld
  • Expectations suddenly change

Changes to how you are treated

  • New or unusual monitoring
  • Exclusion from information needed for your role
  • Public undermining or misrepresentation
  • Sudden criticism with unclear standards
  • Pressure after you raised a concern or set a boundary

Events with a paper trail

  • Accusations or complaints
  • Warnings or performance concerns
  • Approvals and decisions
  • Meetings where responsibility is assigned
  • Praise, outcomes, and completed work
Use significance, not volume.

A short log of specific, consequential events is easier to use than hundreds of entries about minor frustrations.

Purpose

Why keep records before the situation gets bad

Memory gets weaker under pressure, and workplace stories can change quickly. A contemporaneous record gives you dates, details, and source material before you are trying to reconstruct months of events at once.

For protection

  • Preserve instructions and approvals
  • Track when expectations change
  • Keep the sequence of events straight
  • Show the steps you took to clarify or solve the issue

For your future

  • Capture praise before it disappears in old messages
  • Keep measurable project outcomes
  • Track problems you solved
  • Save evidence of leadership and invisible labor
The system

The three documentation files

Keep these as separate categories even if you store them in one spreadsheet or folder.

Evidence file

  • Incidents and dates
  • Exact words when relevant
  • Witnesses
  • Screenshots and messages
  • Pattern links

CYA file

  • Instructions and approvals
  • Scope and deadline changes
  • Recap emails
  • Submitted work and timestamps
  • Proof you followed direction

Wins file

  • Praise and appreciation
  • Results and metrics
  • Problems solved
  • Leadership and extra contribution
  • Evidence of credibility
Why all three?

Your evidence file shows what happened. Your CYA file shows how you handled the work. Your wins file preserves the performance record someone may later minimize or overlook.

In the moment

How to document when you are not at a computer

Use a fast capture system during the day, then expand the entry when you have time. This works in healthcare, education, retail, trades, field work, and other settings where you cannot stop and write a full account.

01
Capture the anchors.

Date, approximate time, location/platform, people present.

02
Write the observable event.

One or two sentences describing what was said or done.

03
Mark the proof.

Screenshot, email, witness, schedule, file, meeting invite, or other record to locate later.

04
Expand later.

Add work impact, your response, and any related earlier incident.

Fast template: Date. Time. Place. People. What happened. Work impact. Proof.
Useful habit: Create the fuller entry the same day when possible, while details are fresh.
Digital proof

Email, chat, screenshots, and records

Digital evidence is strongest when the source, date, participants, and surrounding context are easy to identify.

Email

  • Preserve the relevant thread and date
  • Keep sender and recipients visible
  • Save approvals, deadlines, and changes in direction
  • Use written recaps when a verbal decision needs a record

Slack, Teams, text, or chat

  • Keep names and timestamps visible
  • Preserve enough context to understand the exchange
  • Record the channel or thread
  • Note if messages can be edited or deleted

Writing

How to write a strong entry

Use the same sequence every time. Consistency makes the record easier to review and compare.

01
State the facts.

Who, what, when, and where. Use exact wording when it is important and you can reliably record it.

02
Preserve the proof.

Reference the message, screenshot, document, witness, meeting invite, or other supporting record.

03
Name the work impact.

Explain what changed: delay, access, responsibility, workload, communication, reputation, or ability to complete the work.

04
Record your response.

Clarification requested, recap sent, work completed, concern raised, proof saved, or another step you took.

05
Connect the pattern.

If relevant, identify a previous entry with the same behavior, trigger, person, or consequence.

More useful

  • “At 3:10 p.m., the deadline changed from Friday to Wednesday during the project call.”
  • “I was removed from the client update distribution list on September 8 and missed the revised requirements.”
  • “I sent a written recap at 4:02 p.m. and saved the reply approving the change.”

Harder to use

  • “He is trying to sabotage me.”
  • “She is toxic.”
  • “They always do this.”
Precision

Use behavior language before legal labels

Specific descriptions give the reader something concrete to evaluate. Terms such as discrimination, retaliation, harassment, and hostile work environment can carry legal definitions. Use them carefully and avoid treating the label itself as proof.

Describe the behavior

  • Excluded from information required for the role
  • Standards applied inconsistently
  • Work reassigned without explanation
  • Increased monitoring after a specific event
  • Public statements that misstate responsibility
  • Repeated deadline changes with little notice

Describe the consequence

  • Unable to complete the task by the stated deadline
  • Lost access to information needed for the assignment
  • Responsibility for the delay was attributed to me
  • Required additional work to correct the record
  • Excluded from a decision affecting my responsibilities
Useful sentence starters

“On [date], [person] stated/did…”
“This affected the work by…”
“The supporting record is…”
“I responded by…”
“This appears related to the entry dated…”
“Since [event], I have documented the following change…”

Worked example

Translate the story into a usable record

The goal is to keep the seriousness while removing conclusions the record cannot prove on its own.

First-draft account

“My manager is trying to make me look incompetent. She keeps leaving me out of things and then blaming me when I do not know what changed. Everyone can see what she is doing.”

Documented version

“On September 8, I was not included on the message containing revised requirements for Project A. On September 9 at 10:20 a.m., my manager asked why my draft did not include those requirements. I stated that I had not received them. The revision message shows the recipients and does not include me. This delayed completion by approximately three hours while I updated the draft. A similar omission occurred on September 2; see Entry 09-02.”

What changed?

The second version identifies the event, source, work impact, response, and earlier related incident. The reader can evaluate the pattern without being asked to accept a conclusion first.

Connect the incidents

Pattern tracker

A pattern is more useful when you can describe what repeats and how it changes over time. Review your entries periodically and answer these questions.

What repeats?

  • Same person or group
  • Same type of behavior
  • Same trigger
  • Same consequence
  • Same gap between what was said and what happened

What changed?

  • Frequency increased
  • Behavior spread to more people
  • Scrutiny increased
  • Access or responsibilities decreased
  • Consequences became more serious

Before you save

Records, confidentiality, and legal language

A strong documentation habit also protects the integrity of the records you keep.

Keep control of your record

  • Use a personal system you control when appropriate
  • Keep filenames and dates consistent
  • Preserve original context where possible
  • Back up important records securely

Handle protected information carefully

  • Do not casually remove client, patient, student, customer, trade-secret, proprietary, or other protected information
  • Record what you need without collecting unrelated confidential material
  • Keep private notes separate from a record you may later share
Educational resource

This toolkit provides general educational information about workplace documentation. It does not provide legal advice and does not create an attorney-client relationship. Legal definitions and rules about evidence, recordings, confidential information, protected activity, discrimination, harassment, retaliation, and workplace records vary by jurisdiction and circumstance.

Final documentation test

Can another person tell what happened, when it happened, who was involved, what proof exists, how the work was affected, what you did next, and whether the event connects to a larger pattern? If yes, you have a usable record.
